The aimline and the cone

The portal says 31% today and nothing about yesterday or May. This page puts the number on a line: where it was, the straight line it has to follow to be exam-ready on 14 May, and a cone of where the last four weeks' pace actually lands it. Under the chart, the three signals that say a week stalled before the headline number does. Every figure below is computed from the real maths record as it stood on 25 September.

The questionNot "is 31% good" but "will she get there, and which weeks are the ones to worry about". Both need a line, a target and an honest range.
The unitA step is one topic moving up one level. Maths has 64 topics × 3 levels = 192 steps; 60 are done, 132 to go, 116 maths blocks left before the exam. That is the arithmetic the whole page rests on.
The scienceAn aimline with a decision rule from curriculum-based measurement; a calendar-aware Monte Carlo forecast that resamples her own weeks; flow metrics (touches, age, cycle time) to catch a stall. Nothing fitted, nothing tuned.
Where it lives/s/maths/progress, linked from the "Spec conquered" card; the exam-day figure joins the subject rows on the index; a tracker_progress_forecast tool so the Friday review can quote it.

01

The measure: steps, not a percentage

The 31% is already a sum of points: not started and gap score 0, developing 1, secure 2, exam-ready 3, divided by three per topic. Forecasting in points makes the required pace a whole number a week that a parent can hold in their head, and it can be replayed to any past day from the audit trail, so the line needs no new storage.

192 steps

64 topics × 3 levels. The whole Higher-tier syllabus, exam-ready everywhere.

60 done

24 secure (48) + 9 developing (9) + 1 exam-ready (3). That is the 31%.

132 to go

Over 116 timetabled maths blocks before 14 May: 4.0 a week needed.

3.1 a week

What the last four weeks delivered, per timetabled block, scaled to a week. Close, but two of those weeks were 0 and 2.

Why steps

One step is one visible event on the topic page: "S4 developing → secure". The parent can check any step against its evidence. A percentage of a percentage cannot be argued with; a step can.

Replayed, not stored

The tracker already replays coverage from topic_changes for the week page. The daily series is the same replay at day resolution. Nothing is written; a corrected status corrects the history.

The known skew

Changes are stamped when they were logged, not when the session happened. A session logged the next morning lands its steps a day late. The build should date each change by its session's date where it has one, which the weekly review already does.

How this is worked out
  1. The line is coverage replayed from every status change, one point per day.
  2. The aimline runs from 22% on 19 Aug, the first tracked day, to 100% on exam day. Being under it two weeks running is a warning; four weeks running is the rule to change the teaching.
  3. The cone re-runs the future 5,000 times. Each run first re-draws which of the last four weeks count, then gives every future week the pace of one of those weeks, scaled to how many maths blocks the timetable has that week. Days off carry no blocks. The shaded band is where 70% of runs land; the paler band, 90%.
  4. Stalled is a week with maths blocks and sessions but no steps. Behind is a week that ended under the aimline. Touched, unmoved counts evidence logged against a topic that stayed put.
  5. The cone does not know that later topics may be harder, that exam-ready needs revision-phase work, or about holidays nobody has booked. It narrows as weeks are added.

Summary before detail

Four cards answer the four questions in order: where is she, how fast is she going, where does that land on the day, and when is "everything secure". Each carries its own comparison (three weeks ago, the pace needed, the range, the second goal) so no card is a bare number.

The flag is the stall detector

It only speaks when a rule fires: two weeks under the aimline is amber, four is red, a negative week is red. The wording names the weeks and the cause the record already holds, and points at the week page rather than repeating it.

One chart, three layers

Actual, aimline, cone. The dotted aimline is the target, the dashed median is the projection, the solid line is the fact. Gridlines are hairlines, the only saturated marks are the 2px lines. The 67% "everything secure" rule is the milestone the second card reports.

Provisional, and it says so

Four weeks is the minimum the page will forecast from. Under four it shows the line and the aimline only. The header states the count, and the cone's width is the honest consequence, not a styling choice.

Cost on the server

5,000 runs × 33 weeks is 165,000 draws in PHP, well under 100 ms. The random seed is the date and the subject, so a reload does not redraw the cone and a golden test can pin it.

Real data, one caveat

These figures come from the tracker's history export. Standalone updates carry no session, so the prototype dated them to the nearest session that week; the build reads session_id and gets this exact.

05

How it's worked out

Three bodies of work, each used for the one thing it is good at. None of them is a curve fitted to five points.

1 · The aimline and the four-point rule (curriculum-based measurement)

Progress monitoring in special education has used this since the 1980s: measure a student weekly on the same scale, draw a straight aimline from the baseline to the goal on the goal date, and apply a decision rule. Fuchs, Fuchs and Hamlett's rule is the standard one: four consecutive data points below the aimline means the instruction is not enough and should change; four consecutive above means raise the goal. It was designed for exactly this situation, a handful of noisy weekly points and a parent or teacher who needs a clear trigger rather than a regression coefficient.

Here the scale is steps, the baseline is coverage on the first tracked day, and the goal defaults to 192 steps on exam day. The parent can set a different goal (say 80% by the February mock) and the aimline re-anchors; the rule is unchanged. The page reports the consecutive count under the line and what it means at two and at four.

2 · The cone: a calendar-aware Monte Carlo forecast from her own weeks

Probabilistic forecasting from throughput is the method the flow-metrics community settled on after story-point velocity failed (Vacanti, Actionable Agile Metrics and When Will It Be Done?): never forecast from an average, resample the real history, report percentiles, keep the zero weeks in. Two adaptations make it fit a timetable rather than a Kanban board:

  • The unit of pace is steps per timetabled maths block, not per calendar day. Each past week gives one rate: its steps divided by the blocks the timetable in force that week gave maths. Each future week applies a sampled rate to the blocks it will have, from the timetable version in force and the days off booked. A holiday week has no blocks and so gets no steps; the alternating Thursday deep block is counted on the weeks it falls.
  • Two levels of resampling. With four weeks, the dominant uncertainty is not week-to-week noise but whether 3.1 a week is even her rate. Each run first bootstraps the set of past weeks (draw four with replacement), then draws every future week from that set. The outer draw carries the rate uncertainty, the inner draw the weekly noise. Section 06 shows why the single-level version is not acceptable.

5,000 runs, seeded by date and subject so the page is stable and testable. Outputs: the percentile bands per week, the exam-day distribution, and the date distribution for reaching each milestone, which is where "65% chance of everything secure, typically 28 February" comes from. The cone narrows at roughly the square root of the number of weeks: eight weeks in, it should be about two-thirds as wide.

3 · The stall signals: flow metrics, and process limits once there is enough

  • Steps against the aimline, per week: the CBM rule above.
  • Touches without movement: a status change whose from and to are the same is evidence logged against a topic that stayed put. The ratio of touches to steps is a flow-efficiency measure. Week 37 ran at 1.4 touches a step, week 39 at 13. It does not say the touches were wrong; it says no new topic opened.
  • Work-item age: how long each developing topic has been developing, and how often it has been touched meanwhile. Vacanti's point is that age is the only leading indicator: throughput and the cone tell you about the past, the age of what is open tells you what the next weeks will do. N10 and G14-16 at twelve weeks and five touches each are the two topics to decide about.
  • Cycle time: days from first taught to secure, over topics that made it. Six days typically for a freshly opened topic, ten weeks for the diagnostics-era ones. That is the number that turns "nine in flight" into "expect about a week each, if they are taught".
  • Natural process limits (Wheeler's XmR chart) on the weekly steps: centre line at the mean, limits at ±2.66 × the mean moving range. A week outside the limits is a signal, inside is routine variation. Six weeks is the practical minimum for the limits to mean anything, so the page holds them back until then and says so. With the four weeks in hand the lower limit is below zero and would flag nothing.

4 · The cumulative flow diagram (Kanban)

The five statuses are a workflow: not started → gap → developing → secure → exam-ready. Stacking the counts over time is a cumulative flow diagram, and it is read the same way here as on any board: the width of the yellow band is the work in progress, its horizontal length is how long a topic waits there, the slope of the green edge is throughput. Little's law (time in progress = work in progress ÷ throughput) is what makes "nine in flight at three steps a week" a wait of about three weeks per topic rather than an impression.

What none of it knows

That the 24 unopened topics include the Higher-only ones (surds, circle theorems, vectors, iteration), which will move slower than probability did. That exam-ready is a different kind of work from secure and comes in a revision phase. That regressions cluster around mocks. The cone will learn the first two as they happen; the page says them out loud until then.

Goals

The tracker holds no target grade or target coverage. The default goal (all 192 steps on exam day) is the strictest reading. A parent-set goal per subject, percentage and date, is a two-column addition and a tool argument; the mocks can then be milestones on the chart.

Sources

Fuchs, Fuchs & Hamlett (1990) and Shapiro (2004) on the four-point rule; Vacanti (2015, 2020) on throughput forecasting and work-item age; Wheeler, Understanding Variation, on XmR limits; Little (1961). Links in the footer.

Why the cone is that wide

The same four weeks, four ways of reading them. The first three are the graphs one reaches for first; each gives a confident answer the record cannot support.

The test of a forecast

Not whether it is narrow but whether reality falls inside it as often as it says. A 70% band that holds 70% of the time is the goal; the tracker can score its own past bands once there are months of them, and the page should show that score when it has one.

The line does the arguing

Whatever the cone says, the aimline is a plain fact: 4.0 a week needed, 3.1 delivered, and this week under it. The cone adds "and if that continues, here is May". Either alone is half the page.

Alternatives considered

ApproachVerdictReason
Exponential smoothing with trend (Holt)Later, maybeNeeds a few dozen points to set its two parameters; with four it is a guess wearing a formula. Revisit at 20 weeks as a cross-check on the cone.
Bayesian Gamma–Poisson rate modelRejected for nowThe right shape (a rate with a posterior, overdispersed counts) but it needs a prior for "steps per block" that nobody has, and its answer is harder to explain to the person it is for than "we re-ran her past weeks".
A learning-curve or S-curve fitRejectedEvery fitted curve is a story about the future chosen before the data. The cone lets the data tell it.
Grade projection from mock marksSeparate, not this pageTwo attempts exist. When the mocks arrive their grades belong on this chart as dated marks, not as a trend of their own; the synthesis already refuses to name a grade without a paper.
Per-status transition rates (a Markov model)The upgrade pathModelling gap→developing, developing→secure and secure→exam-ready as separate rates would let the cone know the last third is slower. It needs a term of data for the third rate. The page is built so that the sampler can be swapped without touching the chart.
Only the eight-week sparkline that existsKept, but it is not thisIt shows a shape with no scale, no target and no future. It stays on the week page; this page is what it links to.
08

What it takes to build

Files

  • php/lib/progress.php: pure functions. The daily replay, the per-week block calendar from the timetable versions and days off, the two-level sampler, the aimline and its consecutive count, the weekly table with its flags, ages and cycle times. No HTML, so the tests hit the numbers.
  • php/lib/dashboard_progress.php: the page, in the style of the practice board: heading, SVG, "Chart data" table under every chart. Uses tt_today() throughout so the frozen clock pins it.
  • php/lib/mcp_progress.php: tracker_progress_forecast(subject), the same numbers as text and JSON, for the Friday review and the Saturday synthesis to quote. Read-only.
  • Store: coverageAcross made public and generalised to a day series; two nullable columns on subjects, goal_pct and goal_date, settable through tracker_create_subject.
  • Routes: /s/{slug}/progress; the three one-line entry points; deploy/progress-test.php with a seeded record and pinned clock; a 200 and a 404 in the smoke test; both CI workflows.

Rules the build keeps

  • Under four weeks of blocks: line and aimline only, and the header says how many weeks it has.
  • The seed for the sampler is the date and the slug, so the cone holds still within a day.
  • Changes are dated by their session where they have one; otherwise by when they were logged.
  • Nothing is stored. Correct a status and the whole history re-reads.
09

Verdict, and four questions for you

Build the page as shown. The aimline gives you the plain fact every week (4.0 needed, 3.1 delivered, under the line); the cone gives you May with honest width; the three stall signals name the week and the topic. On the record as it stands, it would already be telling you that week 38 stalled, that N10 and G14-16 have been developing for twelve weeks with five touches each, and that English Literature is three weeks from a number you will not like.

What I would not do is narrow the cone by hand. It is wide because four weeks are four weeks. By half term it will be a different shape on its own.

Q1The goal. Default is every topic exam-ready on exam day. Is that the aimline you want, or "everything secure by the February mock, exam-ready by May" as two milestones? Both can be drawn; the rule needs one.
Q2The window. The cone reads the last eight weeks once it has them. Longer is steadier, shorter reacts faster to a change in teaching. Eight is my recommendation.
Q3Saturday sessions. Session 23 was a Saturday and moved two steps. The prototype treats unscheduled days as carrying no expected steps, so weekend work is a bonus, never assumed. Is that right?
Q4Who sees it. The subject page is public by design. The forecast could be too, or parent-only like the reviews. I would keep the line and the aimline public and gate the cone and the in-flight table, so she sees the target without a probability of missing it.
