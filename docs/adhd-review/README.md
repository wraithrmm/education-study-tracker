# An ADHD-aware review of the GCSE study system

*Prepared 4 September 2026 for the parent who built and runs the system. The single objective it is judged against is the one you gave: help your daughter engage and get through her GCSEs.*

This document is the proposal. The evidence behind it, with every claim's verification status, is in [evidence-table.md](evidence-table.md). Ready-to-paste rewrites of the skill text are in [skill-rewrites.md](skill-rewrites.md). The raw research, verification and review files are under [sources/](sources/).

## 1. The short version

**What the system gets right, and should keep.** The record is honest: evidence is mandatory on every status change, checks are never grade-converted, practice never moves a status, sessions are voided rather than deleted, and one sitting is one attempt. The tutor protocol already has the two best-evidenced learning techniques at its core (retrieval starters and exit tickets, spaced re-tests) and the two rules that matter most for an AI tutor (never hand over the answer during practice, one hint at a time). The "what's one thing you could write down?" script has demonstrably worked: blanks fell from 27 of 39 marks lost on Paper 3F in July to zero on 19 August and zero on 2 September. The no-streaks, no-leaderboards, no-comparison stance is well supported. The Spanish score chart with its personal-best star is the one game-like element in the system that has ADHD-specific evidence behind it.

**What is missing.** The word ADHD appears nowhere in any skill or in the service. The consequences are concrete:

1. **Sessions are built for a neurotypical adult's attention.** Shape A is 80 to 90 unbroken minutes with a 30 to 40 minute teach block, no scheduled break, no daily cap, and no timing said to her. The log already shows the cost: on 2 September two full sessions ran on one day, and every behavioural slip recorded that day (the A4 error that triggered a demotion, both answer-only submissions, the answer-targeting slip, two unfinished practice questions, no exit ticket) happened in the second one.
2. **The behaviours that cost her marks have no home in the data.** "Working not shown" was recorded in four separate places on 2 September, all as free text. Answer-targeting, unfinished questions and arithmetic-in-prose live only in summaries and watch notes. Nothing can count them, trend them, or tell the tutor at session start which habit to target. The one behaviour that does have a column, blanks per paper, is the one that improved.
3. **There is no page for her.** Every page is the tutor's audit record. The subject page opens with a red "252 days to the exam", a coverage figure that stayed at 23% after two full sessions, a "grade below 4" card frozen since July (and wrong: it was a grade 4 on that paper's own series), and an amber warning. The tutor's planned-next line, which answers "what do I do now", is in small italics at the bottom. The chart she likes is not on the Spanish subject page at all.
4. **The plan's numbers do not work, and the plan does not say so.** The December checkpoint expects a Foundation grade 5. That needs 39 of 55 Foundation-tier topics to reach secure in 14 weeks, about 2.7 a week under rules that require two separate touches per topic. The observed rate since the tracker opened is a net loss of one secure topic. The tier intention is Higher on no Higher evidence. Spanish has 1% coverage, 278 days, no confirmed tier, no confirmed centre for the speaking assessment that is 25% of the grade, and the review-queue tool returns nothing actionable for it because every untouched topic is "not started" and there is no sequence.
5. **The exam-logistics dependencies that decide the outcome are not in the system.** Access arrangements for ADHD (supervised rest breaks, a prompter, possibly 25% extra time) need a centre, a specialist's letter, and a documented "normal way of working" that the tracker could produce but does not. A centre must be found that accepts a private candidate for maths, will conduct the Spanish speaking test, and will process access arrangements. Centres set their own entry deadlines, often weeks before the board's. And if the May 2027 maths result disappoints, the November resit series is closed to her: it is restricted to candidates aged 16 or over on 31 August, so the next chance is June 2028.

Two more things the research and the reviews both missed until a final critic named them. Nothing in the system, the skills or this review asks her what she wants, and the adolescent programmes with evidence all find that the young person's own rating of the working alliance predicts who responds. And the safeguarding position of a 13-year-old using Claude and a link-public page that narrates her habits in the third person has not been decided. Both come first in section 5.

## 2. How this review was done, and its limits

- I read the repository, all six GCSE skills and their references, and pulled the live tracker state for both subjects on 4 September 2026.
- Eleven research agents searched the literature across eleven areas (ADHD executive function; durable learning; motivation and gamification; session structure; UK exam logistics; AI tutor design; maths anxiety and technique; Spanish vocabulary and speaking; feedback and praise; self-regulation tools; early entry and tutoring dosage). They produced 151 claims.
- Independent fact-checking agents then tried to refute the claims: 64 were verified (source real, design as claimed, numbers seen), 57 partly verified (source real but a number, year or strength differed; the corrections are shown in the evidence table and applied in this document), 3 not supported (dropped or rewritten here), and 27 non-load-bearing claims came straight from search results and were not independently re-checked. Three areas (feedback, self-regulation tools, early entry) were written from memory because the shared search allowance ran out; every one of their sources was then checked by name, and all exist.
- Seven review agents examined the system through different lenses: the tutoring protocol, the data model, the student-facing pages, the Spanish pipeline, the plan and its arithmetic, engineering feasibility, and the marking outputs. A completeness critic then looked for gaps and contradictions.

Limits you should hold in mind:

- **Web fetching was blocked in this environment.** No paper was read in full; every figure comes from search-result summaries. Where a number matters to a decision I have said what was and was not seen.
- **Most of the learning-science evidence is general-population.** The ADHD-direct evidence for retrieval practice comes from college students learning word lists. No study of spacing, interleaving or worked examples in an ADHD sample was found. Nothing was found on 13-year-olds sitting GCSEs early, or on home-educated students' GCSE outcomes at all.
- **Nothing is known about her individual profile.** Diagnosis detail, medication, comorbidities and what already helps her are unknown to the system and to me. The group-average effects below say where the risks usually lie, not where hers do.
- **The behaviour log is three sessions long.** The 2 September pattern is suggestive, not proof.

## 3. What the evidence says

Evidence strength is graded in brackets: [MA] meta-analysis or systematic review, [RCT], [obs] observational or single lab study, [guide] clinical guideline or official policy, [expert] consensus without trials. "ADHD" marks studies that sampled people with ADHD; everything else is general-population and applied here by inference.

### 3.1 The ADHD profile explains the logged behaviours

- **Working memory.** Children and adolescents with ADHD show large deficits, worst for spatial storage and spatial central-executive tasks (g = 0.85 and 1.06; verbal 0.47 and 0.43) [MA, ADHD, verified]. Multi-step maths done "in the head" is where marks are most likely to go, which is what the recurring A4 sign and collecting-terms slips, and the arithmetic-in-prose habit, look like.
- **Response inhibition.** A medium deficit in stopping an already-prepared response (g = 0.62) [MA, ADHD, verified]. Submitting a bare answer, answering all three colours when only blues were asked, and "skip it" are this signature, not carelessness.
- **Time perception.** Less accurate and less precise judgement of elapsed time (precision deficit g = 0.66, 27 studies) [MA, ADHD, verified]. Her sense of how long a block has taken, or how much exam time is left, cannot be relied on. Nothing in the system records session length or start time.
- **Delay aversion.** A medium preference for small immediate over larger delayed rewards (g = 0.47, 26 studies, 4,320 children) [MA, ADHD, verified]; the effect roughly doubles when the reward is real rather than hypothetical (37 comparisons, N = 3,763) [MA, ADHD, verified]. An exam nine months away is a weak motivator; the next ten minutes are the lever.
- **Persistence.** In a lab study of 30 children with ADHD, strong immediate reinforcement (a sizeable reward, or a computer-game format) normalised task persistence but did not normalise working memory [obs, ADHD, verified]. Engaging formats buy effort, not accuracy: the score chart and the timed game are right to keep, and written working stays necessary alongside them.
- **Vigilance.** Overall sustained-attention accuracy is much lower, but the additional decline over time on task is only small to moderate [MA, ADHD, verified]; a lab study of 96 children found a steeper time-on-task decline in the ADHD group on attention indices, partly offset by reinforcement [obs, ADHD, partly verified]. Lapses happen throughout a session, so "one question, one ask, working every time" matters from minute one, and the case for breaks rests on the next section rather than on a cliff at a fixed minute.

### 3.2 What works for adolescents with ADHD (and what does not)

- **NICE NG87** recommends individually assessed environmental modifications for every child or young person with ADHD: shorter periods of focus with movement breaks (including "I need a break" cards), reinforcing verbal requests with written instructions, and distraction control; medication is to be offered after such modifications have been implemented and reviewed [guide, verified]. NG87 gives no number of minutes.
- **Organisation-skills training** is the best-evidenced non-drug approach for this age group, and the SCCAP evidence-base update rates it well-established for adolescents [MA + systematic review, ADHD, verified]. Effects are moderate to large on organisation (g = 0.54 teacher-rated, 0.83 parent-rated), small to medium on inattention (0.26 and 0.56), and small on academic performance and grades (0.33 and 0.29) [MA, 12 RCTs, 1,054 children, verified]. Expect follow-through to improve before marks do, and do not judge these changes by grade alone.
- **HOPS** (a planner with tasks and dates, materials checklists, work broken into timed segments, a weekly parent check) produced large parent-rated gains in organisation, planning and homework completion in 11 to 14-year-olds (d = 0.88 to 1.05, n = 47) but no teacher-rated change [RCT, ADHD, verified]. Verify progress with the tracker's objective measures, not with impressions of being more organised.
- **Self-regulation interventions** in which the student monitors her own attention or work quality at set intervals show the largest classroom effects in ADHD, though the figures come from single-subject designs and are inflated relative to group trials [MA, ADHD, verified]. A combined effect above 1.0 was found across four intervention types including self-monitoring [MA, ADHD, partly verified: the source does not rank self-monitoring above the others].
- **If-then plans** ("If I want to type skip, then I write one thing I know") brought children with ADHD to the inhibition level of children without ADHD in two randomised lab experiments, and improved delay of gratification in a further pair of studies (n = 45 and 47 + 40) [RCT, ADHD, verified]. Cheap, tested, and untried in a 90-minute tutoring session.
- **Daily report cards** (three to five observable targets reviewed the same day by the parent) improved classroom productivity in a randomised trial of 63 children with ADHD, but the tested version tied the review to home rewards [RCT, ADHD, verified]. A praise-and-review-only version is plausible but is not what was tested. This bears on your no-rewards stance and is a decision for you (section 8).
- **STAND**, parent-teen therapy with motivational interviewing targeting organisation and planning, was acutely efficacious in its trial (n = 128) but showed no overall benefit over usual care when delivered by community therapists (n = 278), with gains only where therapists were licensed [RCT, ADHD, verified]. STAND also includes contingency management. The transferable ingredient is the stance, the student sets goals and chooses the order with the parent monitoring by agreement, not a claimed treatment effect.
- **Not supported:** cognitive "brain-training", neurofeedback and game-based interventions do not reach well-established status; a 2025 meta-analysis of 20 RCTs of game-based interventions in ADHD found gains only on a lab measure of visuospatial short-term memory, not on inhibition or self-monitoring [MA, ADHD, not re-checked]. Growth-mindset interventions have very small average effects on achievement, near zero in the most sceptical synthesis (63 studies, N = 97,672) [MA, verified]; keep only the free elements (process praise, errors as information).

### 3.3 How durable learning works, and where ADHD changes the picture

- **Retrieval practice and spacing** are the only two techniques rated high-utility in the standard review [review, verified]. Spreading retrieval across separate sessions beats massing it (g = 0.74, 29 studies); making the gaps expand is no better than keeping them equal [MA, verified]. Low-stakes classroom quizzing raises achievement (g = 0.50, 222 studies) with larger gains when corrective feedback is given and the quiz format matches the final test [MA, verified].
- **In maths specifically** spacing gives a smaller but reliable benefit (g = 0.28, 27 studies); the evidence for quiz-style retrieval over restudy in maths is described by the authors as inconclusive [MA, partly verified]. The lever in maths is spaced problem-solving, not quizzing facts.
- **Interleaving** worked for seventh-graders, the same age as her: mixed-topic assignments scored 61% versus 38% for blocked practice a month later (d = 0.83, 787 students) [RCT, verified]. Mixing forces the choice of method from the problem, which is exactly what a GCSE paper demands and what answer-targeting undermines. It helps most for confusable pairs (HCF with LCM, area with perimeter, expected with relative frequency) and can hurt for plain word lists, which are better blocked first [MA, not re-checked].
- **The optimal gap** between learning and review is about 20% of the time to the test for tests a few weeks away, falling to 5 to 10% at a year [obs, partly verified: the source's own figures]. For a test 250 days away that implies a gap nearer 10%, roughly 25 days, and for the December mock about two weeks; both are my interpolation from the source's curve. The tracker's "exam-ready after a re-test at three weeks or more" rule sits inside that band; its "ageing at 8 weeks" flag is at the far edge. Because the shape of the schedule barely matters, the load-bearing fix is that developing topics have no schedule at all today, not the exact intervals.
- **Dose.** Practising each new item until it has been recalled three times in the first session, then relearning to one correct recall in about three later spaced sessions, was the efficient prescription in a large study, and applying it in a real course raised exam scores by at least ten percentage points [RCT, verified]. That is a concrete definition of "exam-ready".
- **Worked examples** raise maths performance by a medium amount (g = 0.48, 55 studies), especially with self-explanation prompts, and stop helping once fluency builds [MA, verified]. This is the one place the tutor skill's rule needs qualifying: "never give the answer first" is right for practice and wrong for the first teaching of a new topic, where a worked example she explains back, then a faded example with one step missing, then independent problems, is the evidence-based sequence.
- **ADHD and retrieval.** College students with ADHD gained as much from practice testing as peers (d = 0.50 and 0.57) [RCT, ADHD, verified]. A 2023 study found retrieval helped everyone regardless of ADHD status, but unmedicated participants encoded less, used fewer deep strategies and finished lower, while medicated ones matched controls (36 versus 36, medication status not randomised) [obs, ADHD, partly verified: the study is by Minear and colleagues, not Knouse]. The weakest link is first learning, not testing: the teach block has to secure encoding, because retrieval later will not compensate.
- **Same-day massing.** Seventh-graders who spread the same maths problems across sessions a week apart scored higher and judged their own learning more accurately than those who did them all in one long session, who were overconfident [RCT, not re-checked]. Two full sessions on one day is the massed pattern, and a promotion earned inside the teaching session should be provisional.

### 3.4 Motivation, reward and the no-streak stance

- **Personal-best goals** are associated with achievement and behavioural engagement in students with ADHD at least as strongly as in students without (n = 87 with ADHD, 3,374 without; cross-sectional) [obs, ADHD, partly verified: the ADHD sample is 87, not 3,374]. This is the evidence for the gold star. It is about goals students set, not only records displayed, so the tutor should state a personal-best target at the start of a session, not only show one afterwards.
- **Expected tangible rewards** for doing, finishing or performing well undermine intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28 across 128 experiments), whereas positive informational feedback does not [MA, verified for the undermining figures]. A gold star that marks her own best is informational; it becomes a problem only if it turns into a currency or an expectation.
- **Gamification** has small positive effects on learning (g = 0.49), motivation (0.36) and behaviour (0.25), and only the learning effect holds up in rigorous studies [MA, verified]. Learners value immediate feedback, recognition and goal-setting and dislike comparison and anxiety [MA, verified]. Adding badges and a leaderboard reduced intrinsic motivation and exam scores over a 16-week course [obs, verified]. A logged streak becomes a goal in itself, and a broken one lowers engagement most when the person blames herself [RCT, consumer experiments, verified]. For a student whose inconsistency is a symptom, a streak turns a missed day into a self-attributed failure. The evidence against badges and streaks is one university quasi-experiment and a set of consumer experiments: it shows a real risk in general populations, not proof of harm for this child, and an 8-week randomised trial of a gamified learning app in 80 children with ADHD was published in early 2026 whose results could not be read here and should be checked. **Your stance is well supported on streaks, leaderboards and attendance rewards.** It is over-broad on "nudges": a visible next step, a reachable personal best on a short game, and a reminder she chose herself are scaffolding, not obligation, and the best-evidenced ADHD accommodations are exactly such external cues.
- **Immediacy.** Children with ADHD prefer immediate over delayed reward and respond to reinforcement somewhat more than peers; that they do worse under delayed or intermittent reinforcement is a theoretical prediction rather than a demonstrated difference [review, ADHD, partly verified]. One lab study in adults with ADHD found that delayed feedback let them learn as well as controls where immediate feedback did not, and symptom severity correlated with worse learning from immediate feedback; the delay lengths and sample size were not visible [obs, ADHD, partly verified]. Keep per-item feedback in the game she enjoys; in maths, have her re-read her own line before it is marked. Treat feedback timing as something to test against her data, not a rule.
- **Progress framing.** People persist more when feedback highlights whichever is smaller: progress made early on, progress remaining near the end [RCT, not re-checked]. At 23% and 1% covered, her view should foreground what is secured, not the 77% and 99% to go.

### 3.5 Session structure, breaks and physical state

- **Block length.** No study compares study-block lengths in adolescents with ADHD. The evidence that bears on it: NG87's unquantified "shorter periods of focus with movement breaks" [guide, ADHD, verified]; the steeper time-on-task decline in children with ADHD [obs, ADHD, partly verified]; and an experiment in which university students given pre-scheduled breaks (24 minutes on, 6 off, or 12 on, 3 off) reported less fatigue and distraction and more concentration and motivation than students who chose their own break times, with no loss of task completion [RCT, verified]. Anything between roughly 20 and 40 minutes of focused work followed by a short movement break is consistent with the evidence; 90 unbroken minutes is not. The 24/6 pattern is not shown to beat 12/3, so either is defensible.
- **Exercise.** A single bout of exercise produces a small improvement in executive function in children and adolescents with ADHD (g = 0.17, 12 studies, low-quality evidence); the significant subgroups were bouts of 30 minutes or more at moderate intensity, and bouts of 30 minutes or less were not significant [MA, ADHD, verified with that correction]. Regular exercise programmes improve inattention and executive function over months, with duration mattering more than intensity [MA, ADHD, not re-checked]. Present this as a helpful routine, not a treatment, and do not expect a ten-minute walk before a session to do much on this evidence.
- **Sleep and time of day.** Adolescents with ADHD have more sleep disturbance and a higher share of evening chronotypes than peers, with figures varying widely by study (19% evening in one 2022 clinical sample, 63.7% in a 2025 one) [obs, ADHD, the original claim misattributed the 63.7% figure]. Adolescents perform better on fluid tasks at the time of day matching their chronotype [review, not re-checked]. The practical point is to log session start time, not to assume she is an evening type; the system records neither start time nor sleep, so nobody can see whether weak sessions cluster.
- **Medication.** Nothing here asks about it, but some of the advice silently branches on it: the one study that changed its answer by medication status found retrieval did not repair weak encoding in unmedicated students, and if-then plans worked best combined with stimulant medication. If she takes medication, NG87 describes preparation choice and timing as the prescriber's decisions, titrated against symptoms and adverse effects [guide, partly verified], and expects a shared treatment plan covering educational needs, so whoever manages her ADHD should know she is sitting GCSEs at 13. The only system-level implication is indirect: if start times are logged, you can see whether the hardest sessions fall inside the window agreed with the clinician. Medication stays out of the tracker's structured data and out of what Claude asks her.
- **Body doubling** is widely valued by neurodivergent people but the evidence is a 2023 survey and small adult experiments [obs, ADHD, not re-checked]. Cheap to try, not something to write into a skill as a rule.

### 3.6 Maths: anxiety, technique and blanks

- **Maths anxiety** correlates negatively with performance in school-age students (r = -0.34 across 131 studies; -0.27 at primary, -0.36 at secondary) [MA, verified], and a second large meta-analysis puts it at about r = -0.28 [MA, not re-checked]. Undergraduates with an ADHD diagnosis or traits (n = 40 versus 163) reported higher maths anxiety than peers even after controlling for general anxiety [obs, ADHD, partly verified: the "equal arithmetic" half of the original claim was not seen]. Anxiety disorders co-occur with ADHD in 18.4% of children and adolescents (121 studies, N = 39,894) [MA, ADHD, verified]. Where anxiety is confirmed, interventions have a pooled effect of g = 0.50 on maths performance and -0.47 on anxiety (50 studies) [MA, verified]. Nothing in the system records how anxious she is; a one-item optional self-rating before timed work would let you see whether blanks track anxiety or skill gaps.
- **Strategy instruction.** Ten days of explicit "plan before you compute" instruction, randomised by classroom, produced worksheet gains of 0.85 versus 0.26 for the comparison group in students with ADHD and learning disabilities, with transfer to standardised maths tests and an advantage still present a year later [RCT, ADHD, partly verified: the transfer effect sizes, the sample size and the pupils' ages were not visible, so the fit to a 13-year-old is unconfirmed]. A fixed plan-do-check routine is still the highest-value teaching change for the answer-targeting and sign-slip patterns, because it also follows directly from the inhibition and working-memory evidence.
- **Blanks.** In a high-school sample (38 with ADHD, 746 without), students with ADHD attempted as many items and reported similar test anxiety as peers but were less accurate, on a reading battery rather than maths [obs, ADHD, verified]; leaving answers blank is a modifiable behaviour, as her own record shows. The next metric is marks lost on attempted questions. And the marker review found something neither output file says: on the July Paper 2 script, nine of her eleven blanks sit between questions she got fully right, so they were skips, not time. Only the last two look like time.
- **Method marks.** AQA's mark-scheme glossary says examinations are marked "to award positive achievement wherever possible", with M marks for a correct method and credit for any valid method, so an attempted question can never score below a blank [policy, verified]. The "one thing you could write down" script rests on this.

### 3.7 Spanish: vocabulary, speed pressure, listening and speaking

- **Spacing for vocabulary.** Repeated retrieval spread over long intervals produced a 200% improvement in long-term retention compared with unspaced repetition, and the shape of the schedule made no difference [RCT, verified]; expanding gaps beat equal gaps only by a very small margin in second-language word learning [RCT, not re-checked]. A per-word "next due" date matters; the algorithm does not. Days of use of a spaced-repetition app predicted end-of-semester Spanish performance in 62 beginners, controlling for ability and motivation, and the students disliked the app [obs, verified]: a short daily touch beats a weekly block, and adherence is the known failure mode.
- **Speed pressure.** Children and adolescents with ADHD (25 versus 30 controls) are impaired at optimising the speed-accuracy trade-off, with a "relative inflexibility in optimising decision criteria" [obs, ADHD, partly verified: the link to hyperactive-impulsive symptoms specifically was not seen]. The Shooting Gallery speeds up every three correct answers; its 71 to 81% accuracy should not be read as vocabulary knowledge, and with three options the chance-corrected figure is nearer 65%. Keep the ramp she enjoys, but offer a steady mode, log timeouts separately from wrong picks, and confirm knowledge untimed before it counts.
- **The defined vocabulary list.** The AQA 8692 specification prescribes 1,200 lexical items at Foundation plus 500 more at Higher; up to 2% of a text may be off-list cognates, off-list proper nouns may be glossed, single off-list words may appear for inference in written texts, and dictation includes some off-list vocabulary [policy, partly verified against the specification]. The exam vocabulary is a closed set; every app set should be tagged against it, Foundation list first.
- **First exams were summer 2026** (first teaching September 2024), so a June 2027 candidate has exactly one real past series plus specimen materials [policy, verified]. A 2025 reissue of the DfE subject content surfaced in search and should be checked for changes.
- **Chatbots and speaking.** Adding an AI chatbot to speaking activities over six weeks reduced speaking anxiety and improved speaking performance in a quasi-experimental study of 75 university students [obs, verified], and a systematic review finds lowering affective barriers is chatbots' most consistent benefit [review, not re-checked]. But when 48 students sat one AI-facilitated and one human-facilitated speaking exam, their scores did not differ; what differed was that anxiety depressed scores only in the human-facilitated exam [obs, verified: the original claim misstated this]. Lower anxiety with a chatbot is no guarantee it holds with a human examiner, so human rehearsals with you from spring 2027 are needed alongside the chat.
- **Contextual knowledge.** Spacing improves the explicit, recallable vocabulary that flashcards and translation test, not the tacit knowledge that supports fluent reading, which needs repeated exposure in context [RCT, not re-checked]. Reading and listening need short passages that recycle the list words; the app has neither.

### 3.8 Feedback, praise and emotional regulation

- **Process praise, never person praise.** Fifth-graders praised for intelligence chose easier tasks, persisted less after failure and almost 40% misreported their scores; process praise did the opposite [RCT, verified; a classic single-laboratory study whose replication record was not checked]. Inflated praise ("amazing!") is given about twice as often to children with low self-esteem and increases their avoidance of challenge [RCT, verified]. Large language models default to superlatives; a banned-word list belongs in every skill.
- **Feedback levels.** Task-, process- and self-regulation-level feedback works; feedback aimed at the self is least effective, and about a third of feedback interventions in the classic meta-analysis reduced performance [review, verified]. Feedback's overall effect is d = 0.48 across 435 studies, larger for cognitive than motivational outcomes and larger when it carries more information [MA, verified]. Corrections should say what the question asked, what her working shows, and the single next move.
- **Emotional dysregulation** is present in roughly 25 to 45% of children with ADHD and is a major driver of impairment [review, ADHD, verified via secondary citations]. "I can't", "skip it", one-word replies and sudden quitting deserve a scripted, neutral de-escalation and a return on a guaranteed win, not a hint. "Rejection sensitive dysphoria" is not a validated diagnosis and should not appear in skills or on pages [verified].
- **Mastery experiences** are typically the strongest source of academic self-efficacy [review, verified]: sequence every session to succeed early and end on a success.
- **Response cost.** A recalled claim that taking points away for errors escalates conflict was not supported by its cited source, and the reinforcement review actually reports response cost improving performance in children with ADHD. I have dropped that claim. The defensible position is simply your existing one: nothing is withheld for a missed day.

### 3.9 AI tutoring: what the trials say

- Step-based intelligent tutoring is nearly as effective as human one-to-one tutoring (d = 0.76 versus 0.79, pre-LLM systems, mostly college samples) [MA, verified]; across 50 controlled evaluations the median gain is 0.66 SD, and it depends "to a great extent" on whether it is measured on the system's own tests or standardised ones [MA, verified]. Exit tickets will overstate exam readiness; only timed real papers should drive the December and February decisions.
- **Guardrails are load-bearing.** In a field experiment with nearly a thousand high-school maths students, unguarded ChatGPT access raised practice scores by 48% but left students scoring 17% worse than controls on the unassisted exam; a version that gave teacher-designed hints and withheld answers raised practice scores by 127% and "largely" (not fully) removed the harm [RCT, verified]. In a lab experiment with 117 students, a ChatGPT group improved essay scores more but showed no greater knowledge gain or transfer, and the authors named "metacognitive laziness" as a risk rather than a measured outcome [obs, partly verified: the original claim overstated it]. The guardrail bundled hints, guidance and answer-withholding, so the effect cannot be attributed to withholding alone. The "never give the answer first" rule must also govern the Spanish app's AI chat, an exit ticket answered with help should not count towards secure, and a predicted-versus-actual score before each exit ticket is the cheapest early warning of an illusion of mastery. None of these samples resembles hers, so this review quotes no expected gain for Claude as a tutor: the December mock is the system's first real test.
- A purpose-built LLM tutor with expert-authored lesson content beat a well-run active-learning class: 194 Harvard students learned more than twice as much in less time, with higher engagement [RCT, verified]. The design that won fed vetted content to the model; per-topic worked-example and misconception notes for Claude are the analogue here.
- **Adolescents and AI.** The APA's June 2025 advisory states adolescents are less likely than adults to question the accuracy and intent of information from a bot, warns that AI can substitute for human connection, and directs its recommendations at parents and educators [policy, verified]. Keep Claude's role academic, keep sessions reviewable, and tell her plainly that Claude can be wrong and the mark scheme settles disagreements. Consumer AI services commonly require users to be 18 or older; she should be using Claude through your supervised account and the skills [not verified by search; check the terms].
- **Interface guidance** for cognitive accessibility (W3C COGA, CAST UDL) and ADHD practitioner sources converge on one task per screen, the current instruction kept visible, progress markers, and a written next step at the end of every turn [guide and expert]. None of it is trial evidence.

### 3.10 Exam logistics for a home-educated private candidate with ADHD

All of this is policy, verified against JCQ and AQA pages via search summaries. Re-check the 2026-27 JCQ regulations (effective 1 September 2026) before acting; only the 2025-26 edition with its March 2026 update was visible.

- **Access arrangements.** For a need arising from ADHD, JCQ's route to 25% extra time is Form 9 backed by specialist medical evidence (CAMHS, an HCPC-registered psychologist, a consultant, a psychiatrist, a speech and language therapist, or a GP who has completed the RCGP extended-role framework in ADHD); a referral alone is not enough. Since the March 2026 revision the SENCo "should consider" supervised rest breaks before applying for extra time (the September 2025 wording required a documented trial; that stricter rule was reversed). Supervised rest breaks and a prompter are centre-delegated, need no online application, and JCQ's own worked example for them is a candidate with ADHD. Separate invigilation is a SENCo decision requiring evidence of a long-term condition; nervousness is not sufficient. Every route requires evidence that the arrangement is her normal way of working, and JCQ's guidance for centres accepting private candidates says the SENCo should refer to online feedback given while studying and to reported difficulties from regular 1:1 tuition. That is exactly what the tracker holds, in prose.
- **Deadlines.** The JCQ deadline for access-arrangement applications for June 2027 is 21 March 2027 (modified papers 31 January 2027). AQA's own June 2027 entry deadline was not confirmed by search (the appendices assume about 21 February); one London private-candidate centre publishes 1 February 2027 at £290 per subject, £425 late. AQA charges 100% extra for late entry and 150% for very late. Do not commission a private educational-psychologist assessment before a centre is engaged: JCQ says one carried out without prior consultation with the centre cannot be used.
- **Centre.** JCQ and AQA both publish finders and JCQ warns that listed centres may no longer accept private candidates, so confirm directly. The AQA news guide that appears in search is from 2021 and its dates do not apply. The centre must say yes to three things: private candidates for maths, conducting the Spanish speaking test (AQA's policy: centres must only accept a private-candidate entry if they are willing to provide the NEA), and processing access arrangements for an external candidate.
- **Age.** There is no minimum age for GCSE entry. The November series in maths and English language is restricted to candidates aged 16 or over on 31 August of that year, so a disappointing May 2027 result cannot be improved until June 2028.

### 3.11 Early entry, hours and tutoring dosage

- DfE's 2011 analysis found early GCSE entry associated with lower grades and led to the 2013 rule that only first entries count for schools; the mechanism was school-driven mass entry, and the accountability rule does not touch a private candidate's own certificate [policy, partly verified]. The transferable lesson is only: do not sit before coverage is complete.
- A full GCSE is assigned 120 to 140 guided learning hours by the awarding body (AQA maths appears to be 140) [policy, partly verified]. From now to the exams that is roughly 4 to 4.5 tutored hours a week for maths and about 3.7 for Spanish, before independent practice. Nothing records hours, so you cannot see whether the plan is on budget.
- One-to-one tuition in the EEF toolkit averages five months' progress, but mathematics is two months and literacy six; the best results come from short regular sessions, about 30 minutes three to five times a week over up to ten weeks [review, partly verified]. The large tutoring meta-analysis reported 0.37 SD in its working paper and 0.288 SD in the peer-reviewed version, with larger effects for trained tutors than for parents or volunteers and none of it involving AI tutors [MA, partly verified]. Neither figure can be assumed to transfer to Claude.
- Self-regulated learning training improves achievement at secondary level (d = 0.54) with the maths advantage a primary-school finding only [MA, partly verified]. Embed short planning and checking routines inside the subject work rather than as a separate study-skills module.
- There is no national dataset of home-educated children's GCSE results [partly verified]. Her own mocks against published boundaries are the only benchmark.

## 6. Things I recommend not doing

- **Do not build mindset modules, posters or repeated "your brain can grow" messages.** The evidence is small at best and near zero in the most sceptical synthesis. Keep the free parts.
- **Do not divert study time to brain-training or attention games.** They do not transfer to schoolwork on the ADHD evidence.
- **Do not add streaks, leaderboards, attendance rewards or cross-subject comparison.** Your stance is right. Narrow the README's wording so it stops barring supportive cues.
- **Do not pay per mark or per topic secured, but do not read the ADHD evidence as anti-reward either.** The board stance (nothing withheld, no streaks, no comparison) is supported. A no-home-rewards reading is not: every ADHD-direct package cited (the daily report card, HOPS, STAND, the persistence study) used tangible, immediate, parent-agreed contingencies for behaviours. The undermining effect in the general-population meta-analysis is for expected rewards contingent on performance at tasks people already find interesting. If you ever tie home privileges to study, tie them to sessions started, questions submitted and working shown, never to accuracy, and keep them small and pre-agreed with her.
- **Do not treat "largely autonomous" as compatible with the evidence.** Every well-established package includes an adult checking weekly. The fix is weekly, collaborative and visible to her, not daily inspection and not a behaviour log she did not choose.
- **Do not build parent-facing admin features before her page.** The checklist, the digest and the milestones schema all duplicate a dashboard you already have.
- **Do not build a sophisticated spaced-repetition algorithm.** Expanding schedules are no better than equal ones; a fixed ladder captures the benefit.
- **Do not treat exit tickets as exam evidence.** Tutoring-system gains look larger on the system's own tests. Only timed real papers decide December and February.
- **Do not put medication in the tracker or in Claude's questions.** Log start times; take the pattern to the clinician.
- **Do not use "rejection sensitive dysphoria" anywhere.** Design for emotional dysregulation, which is real; do not self-label with a construct that is not validated.
- **Do not commission a private educational-psychologist assessment before a centre is engaged.** JCQ will not accept it. For ADHD the medical letter is the route anyway.
- **Do not run two full sessions in a day.** The record already shows what that does.
- **Do not let the headline number be the countdown.** For her, a distal deadline is nearly invisible and a red one reads as "late".
