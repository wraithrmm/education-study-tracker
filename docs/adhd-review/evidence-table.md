Researcher caveats:
- The session-wide WebSearch budget was exhausted after 29 searches; four planned searches were refused: Dekkers et al. 2017 on time-on-task effects in ADHD (surfaced at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5701950/ but not read), the Sibley et al. 2025 JCPP Research Review on adolescent ADHD treatments (https://acamh.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcpp.14056?af=R, content not shown), the maths error-types study in adolescents with ADHD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5641671/), and a procrastination-ADHD association study.
- Task initiation and procrastination: no meta-analysis, RCT or clinical study specific to adolescents with ADHD appeared in results; the search returned practitioner blogs and one 2005 study on executive and motivational processes in adolescents with ADHD (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1183187/). The delay-aversion literature (C3) is the nearest mechanism-level evidence and the claim about 'starting is the hardest step' is an inference from it.
- Time-on-task decline (C5): Huang-Pollock et al. reported only a 'handful' of studies with performance-over-time data and no exact g was shown in the search summary. No study of optimal study-session length for adolescents with ADHD was found; NICE says 'shorter periods of focus' without a number, so the 20-25 minute figure in C8's implication is a design suggestion, not a guideline value.
- Maths error link used in C1's implication: 'Working Memory and Math Skills in Children With and Without ADHD' (https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/neu-neu0000920.pdf) and 'Neurocognitive and Behavioral Predictors of Math Performance in Children with and without ADHD' (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3966972/) report that working-memory deficits partially account for ADHD-related maths achievement deficits and describe procedural, carrying/borrowing and multistep errors and rapid responding; one study found no ADHD difference in maths accuracy; numbers not shown in search summaries.
- All intervention trials found (HOPS, CHP, STAND, OST meta-analysis) are US school- or clinic-based with trained providers, children aged roughly 8-17, and no home-educated participants and no AI tutoring; transfer to a parent plus Claude-skill setting is inference. STAND's null community result shows delivery quality matters.
- CHP: the CrimeSolutions programme summary states the 2016 main trial found no statistically significant differences in academic functioning or parent/teacher ADHD ratings vs control, while the trajectories paper shows heterogeneous response; treat CHP as evidence about who responds, not as evidence of reliable academic gain. Primary effect sizes were not shown in any search summary.
- Evans et al. 2018 detail that 'combined training' is Level 2 (probably efficacious) came from a search summary that may have blended sources; the well-established status of organisation training for adolescents was consistent across summaries. A search summary attributed to Sibley et al. 2023 (Lancet Child & Adolescent Health) the statement that OST is well-established for adolescents and mindfulness-based interventions are newly supported; not verified.
- Gaastra et al. 2016 (C14): the SMD of 3.61 is from single-subject designs and is not comparable to group-design effect sizes; the underlying self-monitoring studies are small.
- NICE NG87 update history: the brief says '2018, updated 2019'; search results show further updates in January 2023 (ADHD described as a neurodevelopmental disorder, ICD-11, parent support to be group-based and ADHD-focused) and May 2025 (link to diagnostics guidance). NG87 does not cover exam access arrangements (extra time, rest breaks, prompter), which are a JCQ process outside this area key.
- All executive-function meta-analyses (C1-C6) sample children and adolescents with diagnosed ADHD and give group-average effects; nothing in the system records this student's individual profile, diagnosis detail, medication status or comorbidities, so which of these deficits apply to her, and how strongly, is unknown. Several trials include medicated participants; effects of non-pharmacological measures may differ by medication status.
- Dovis et al. 2012 (C7) is a single lab experiment with 30 ADHD participants using monetary and game incentives; it supports the direction (reinforcement restores persistence) but not any particular size of effect for a score chart or timed game.

Researcher caveats:
- ADHD-specific evidence for durable-learning techniques is thin: the only direct studies found are Knouse and colleagues' work with college students (word lists, key-term definitions, text recall) and Kasper et al. 2012 on working memory in children. No study of spacing, interleaving or worked examples in an ADHD sample was found, and nothing about a 13-year-old home-educated learner; claims C1-C10 and C13 are general-population and their application to this student is inference.
- The 2025 maths meta-analysis (Murray, Horner & Goebel) found the testing-vs-restudy effect in maths was not robust (g = 0.18, CI crossing zero), which conflicts with the general testing-effect literature (Adesope 2017; Yang 2021, g = 0.499). Treat retrieval practice in maths as 'do spaced problem-solving' rather than 'quiz on facts'.

A protocol for active breaks in 10-12-year-olds with and without ADHD (Break4Brain, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11583342/) is registered but had no results in the search results.
- Body doubling: a general-audience source in the results states the evidence is anecdotal with no controlled research testing effectiveness; a blog-style summary reported 27-30% faster task completion in a 2025 VR experiment, but no primary source URL for that figure appeared, so it is not used in the claims.
- Sleep and chronotype figures came from an aggregated search summary: the 63.7% evening chronotype, a '62 studies, 4,462 patients' systematic review count, and a '73-78% delayed sleep/wake cycle' figure (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12728042/) could not each be tied with certainty to the exact source that states them. Direction of the finding is consistent across sources; the precise numbers should be re-checked before quoting.
- Time-of-day evidence (C11) is general-population adolescent research, not ADHD-specific, and applies most clearly to fluid tasks. Preckel et al. 2011 (general population) found eveningness correlated negatively with academic achievement (r = -0.14, N = 700) and morningness positively (r = 0.16, N = 3220), probably reflecting schedule mismatch rather than ability: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S104160801100080X
- Doing two long sessions in one day: no direct study was found. The recommendation against it is an inference from the spacing literature (C4) and from time-on-task decline (C1, C2), not from an ADHD trial.
- Medication (C14) is cited as guidance only. Whether the student takes medication is unknown, and nothing here should be read as advice on dosing or timing; those are for the prescriber.
