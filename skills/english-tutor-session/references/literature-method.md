# Teaching AQA English Literature 8702 — the method

Read when the session is Literature. Everything here is built from what AQA's mark schemes and
examiner reports reward and penalise, adapted for a student who works best in short, concrete,
single-focus steps.

## The exam, in one screen

| Paper | Section | Text | Marks | Extract? | Time guide |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (1h45, 64 marks, 40%) | A Shakespeare | one of six plays | 30 + **4 AO4** | Yes — analyse extract, then whole play | 50–55 min |
| 1 | B 19th-century novel | one of seven | 30 | Yes — extract, then whole novel | 50 min |
| 2 (2h15, 96 marks, 60%) | A Modern text | one of twelve | 30 + **4 AO4** | **No** — essay from memory, choice of two questions | 45 min |
| 2 | B Anthology poetry | one cluster of 15 | 30 | One named poem printed; she picks the second | 45 min |
| 2 | C Unseen poetry | — | 24 + 8 | Both poems printed | 30 + 15 min |

Closed book throughout. **The modern-text essay is the only pure-recall section** — weight the
quotation retrieval there. AO4 (SPaG, 4 marks) is assessed only in Shakespeare and the modern text.

AO weightings across the GCSE: AO1 37.5% · AO2 42.5% · AO3 15% · AO4 5%. **AO2 — how the writer
does it — is the biggest single share.** Teaching that neglects methods caps every grade.

## Phase order for a new text

1. **Story first (2–3 sessions).** Read/listen to the whole text (audiobook + text together works
   well for sustained attention). Build a one-page map: plot in ten beats, five characters in one
   line each, four themes with where each shows up. No analysis yet.
2. **Quotation bank (built across sessions).** 8–12 quotations, chosen by her, each ≤ 12 words,
   each tagged: two themes, one character, one "method" word (imagery / repetition / stage
   direction / structure / contrast). One quote should serve several questions — that's the test
   for keeping it. Store the bank as a resource on the tracker topic.
3. **Methods on the map.** For each theme, *how* does the writer show it? Structure (where it
   sits in the text, what changes), language (image patterns, word choice), form (stage
   directions, narrative voice, verse/prose). This is the AO2 content.
4. **Context as clauses.** For each theme, one sentence of context that ends in "…which is why
   [writer] [does X]". Nothing longer. Bolt-on context paragraphs are the most-cited examiner
   complaint across boards.
5. **Paragraphs → essays.** What–how–why paragraphs one at a time; then two linked paragraphs; then
   a full timed essay in Shape C.

## The what–how–why paragraph (AO1 → AO2 → AO3)

- **What:** a claim that answers the question, using the question's key word. "Priestley
  presents Birling as complacent…"
- **How:** the evidence — a short embedded quote or a close reference — *and the method*. "…through
  dramatic irony when he calls the Titanic 'unsinkable'…"
- **Why:** the effect and the purpose. "…so that the 1945 audience, who know what happened, hear
  every confident prediction as a warning about men like him."
- **Then:** zoom in on one word or structural choice for a second layer, or link to elsewhere in
  the text ("this pattern returns when…").

The descriptor ladder she is climbing, in plain words:
- Level 3 (11–15): explained, some relevant references, methods identified.
- Level 4 (16–20): **clear** and **explained**, effects of methods explained, context linked.
- Level 5 (21–25): **thoughtful, developed**, references integrated, methods analysed, context
  explored.
- Level 6 (26–30): **convincing, critical, exploratory**, judicious references, fine-grained
  analysis, conceptualised argument.

The move from 4 to 5 is *development*: taking one point further rather than adding another point.
Teach "say more about the same thing" before "say another thing".

## Extract questions (Shakespeare, 19th-century novel)

Routine: (1) read the question and underline its key word; (2) read the extract once for sense,
once marking three moments that answer the question; (3) write two paragraphs on the extract,
close to the words; (4) write two paragraphs on the rest of the text, from the bank; (5) one
sentence of conclusion that answers the question directly. The extract is the launchpad, and the
rule is hard: an answer that never leaves the extract, or never uses it, misses a defining
feature of the task and is **capped at Level 2 (10/30)** by the examiner. Two paragraphs each way
is the safe shape.

## Modern text (no extract)

Choice of two questions — teach her to pick in under a minute by asking "which has more of my
bank?". Plan = three theme-words + one quote each, written in the answer booklet before the essay.
This is the section where a blank costs 30 marks; the plan-first rule exists for it.

## Anthology poetry (comparison)

- Fifteen poems; one is printed, she chooses the partner. **Both are required** — an answer on the
  named poem alone is capped at Level 2 (10/30), so the partner-poem habit is non-negotiable. Pre-teach **pairings by theme** (three
  or four "if this comes up, I compare it with…" for each poem) so the choice is instant.
- Structure: point about both poems → poem A evidence and method → poem B evidence and method
  → what the comparison shows. Comparative connectives every paragraph ("whereas", "similarly",
  "both… but").
- She knows the printed poem well and must recall the second: the bank for poems is two quotes
  plus one structural fact per poem.

## Unseen poetry — the fixed routine

Q1 (24 marks, one poem): **read twice**. Then in order: one sentence on what it's literally
about; one on the feeling/tone; three moments where the poet's choices create that feeling (word,
image, structure, sound); write three what–how–why paragraphs from those moments. No context
needed — AO3 isn't assessed here.

Q2 (8 marks, compare with a second poem): **methods only**, two short paragraphs — a similarity in
method and a difference in method, each with a quote from both poems. Don't restart the analysis
of poem one; build on Q1.

## Retrieval starters (Literature)

Mix, five questions, ~5 minutes:
- "Who says '…' and what's happening?"
- "Give me a quote for [theme] in [text] and the method in it."
- "One sentence: why does [writer] include [character/scene]?"
- "Which two anthology poems would you pair for [theme]?"
- "What's the structural shape of [poem]?"

Score them, log the score as evidence, move on. A miss goes on the `watch` note, not into a lecture.

## Common examiner-report complaints to design against

- Feature-spotting without effect ("this is a simile").
- Bolted-on context; context that swamps the answer; "audiences would have been shocked".
- Narrative retelling instead of analysis.
- Treating characters as real people rather than the writer's constructs — the subject of the
  sentence is *the writer*, not the character.
- Long quotations that don't get analysed; inaccurate quotations in quotation marks.
- Not answering the question set; not reading the question's key word.
- Confusing the poet with the poem's speaker.
